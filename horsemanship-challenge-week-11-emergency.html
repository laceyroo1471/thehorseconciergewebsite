    Tuesday November 10

    Calling 911

    Prepare a clear location, a callback number, and an accurate description of the scene—then practice without placing a call.

    Content unlocks Tuesday, November 10.

    Make the call easier before you need it

    An emergency call is a conversation, not a speech you have to get perfect. Be ready to give the incident location, your callback number, and a short description of what has happened. Answer the call-taker’s questions, follow instructions, and stay on the line until told to hang up.

    For a horse emergency, prepare a plain-language description of what you can see from a safe place: how many horses are involved, whether the horse is standing or lying down, whether it appears trapped, and any danger to people. Describe observations rather than deciding which rescue technique is needed. A horse’s position and mobility help responders understand the scene; they do not let a caller determine sedation or the order agencies should arrive.

    Give the location where help is needed, not just your home address. Include the barn entrance, nearest intersection, landmarks, or trail markers if relevant. Do not assume a mobile phone pin will take responders to the correct gate.

    Call 911 for an incident requiring immediate police, fire, or ambulance assistance; contact your veterinarian for the horse’s medical care. Local dispatch and the attending veterinarian can help coordinate the appropriate resources. A request for animal technical rescue is not a guarantee that a trained team is nearby.

    Read the National 911 Program calling guide

    Location and call-preparation guidance from Dayton public safety

    Optional · no additional points

    Try it on paper

    Write a private call card with:

    • The incident address and entrance.
    • A callback number.
    • A short scene description.
    • Your veterinarian’s contact information.
    • Any access issue responders should know.

    Practice describing a fictional incident aloud without making a call. Keep gate codes and private contact information off public posts.

    Before an emergency, use your local agency’s published non-emergency contact to ask how animal technical rescue is coordinated in your area. Do not call 911 to rehearse, test a location, or ask routine planning questions.

    Wednesday November 11

    Barn Fires

    Latches, exits, and the route you would actually use — then walk it, and name two hazards in your own barn. What’s It Weigh Wednesday opens today.

    Content unlocks Wednesday, November 11.

    What to watch for

    Go past the obvious flammables. Notice how fast smoke moves, latches that take two hands, exits a responder cannot find, and the gathering point you would actually reach.

    Walk your evacuation route · 5 points

    This is a baseline, not a race. Start where you would actually be. Halter the horse. Follow the exit you would use. Stop at the gathering point.

    Track it with GPS in the app during this week. In the activity notes, write the wasted steps or delays you noticed. The note is what completes the 5 points.

    Two fire hazards · 5 points

    Name two things in your barn that would slow a fire evacuation or a responder. A photo is welcome. The two written hazards are what earn the points. Photos together need to stay under 10 MB.

    Friday November 13

    Entrapments and Rescue

    Recognize when a trapped or down horse needs specialized help, and plan how to reach the right resources before an emergency.

    Content unlocks Friday, November 13.

    Know when this is more than a handling problem

    Animal technical rescue involves specialized equipment and training beyond a standard emergency response. Examples include horses trapped below ground level, animals in overturned trailers, confined spaces, and flood or swiftwater incidents. Being familiar with a horse does not provide the training needed to manage those hazards.

    If a horse is trapped or cannot get up, contact your veterinarian promptly. When the incident requires immediate fire, police, or ambulance assistance, call 911 as well. Describe the horse’s location, position, visible condition, and surrounding hazards from a safe place. Follow the instructions of the responding professionals.

    Flood rescue is especially dangerous. UC Davis warns that rescuing submerged large animals requires a properly trained and equipped swiftwater team, with veterinary or knowledgeable livestock support. Do not enter floodwater to reach a horse or attempt an improvised extraction. If a horse will not move after a flood, call your veterinarian rather than forcing it.

    Read the UF VETS technical rescue overview

    UC Davis flooding guide—see the Respond section

    Build the contact chain before the emergency

    UF VETS recommends planning with local emergency managers, public safety agencies, and community partners before an incident. Identify who coordinates animal rescue locally, how they are contacted, and which resources may be available. A list of names is useful only if the contacts are current and the people involved understand their roles. Local rescue availability and activation procedures vary.

    Read the UF VETS response-planning guide

    Optional · no additional points

    Think it through

    Scenario: a horse has slipped into a flooded ditch and cannot get out. From a safe location, what would you report, who would you contact, and how would you keep other people from entering the hazard?

    Write a short response privately. Do not recreate the scene or practice rescue techniques on a horse.

    Your role is to recognize the need for specialized help, communicate clearly, and avoid adding another casualty.

    Discussion

    Do you know how animal technical rescue is coordinated where your horse lives?

    Saturday November 14

    Week Review

    Five questions from this week. Each correct answer is 4 points.

    Content unlocks Saturday, November 14.

    Five questions · 4 points each

    Stay signed in. Review the lessons and readings before answering. A wrong answer does not award points, and you will see the right one.

    1. Emergency handoff. An owner is hospitalized and cannot communicate. Their designated caregiver discovers that a horse’s medication instructions in the app differ from the prescription label. What is the best next step?

    2. Calling 911. A horse is trapped in a drainage ditch. You have called 911, provided the location, and described the situation. The dispatcher asks whether the horse is standing or lying down. Why might this information matter?

    3. Barn fire. A barn has smoke detectors, accessible exits, and halters near every stall. During a walkthrough, you discover that several stall latches require two hands and a precise lifting motion. What is the primary concern?

    4. Emergency evacuation. A horse owner has confirmed transportation for all six horses and identified an evacuation destination. What additional issue could make the plan unworkable?

    5. Horse entrapment and rescue. A horse is trapped below ground level in a flooded drainage channel. Which feature makes this an animal technical rescue incident rather than a routine handling problem?
